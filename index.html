Three things. Check your recall

The three you have studied. Plato, through Socrates and Diotima, defines love as desire for something one lacks and finally for the good to be one's own for ever (Symposium 200a to 206a). Sappho does not define it; she reports what it does: "Love which loosens the limbs Once again shakes me! Bitter-sweet, invincible creature that he is" (Fragment 130). Ovid's teacher treats love as a technique: "love lasts long through art" (Ars 3.42) and his lessons are in "the ways of being loved" (3.28).

Seneca. A Roman Stoic philosopher, tutor and adviser to Nero, forced to kill himself in AD 65. Stoicism holds that virtue is the only good and that the passions, including desire (cupiditas), are mistaken judgements the mind assents to. So he will not say "desire is bad because it is strong"; he will say desire is a false judgement that something external is worth wanting, and that it can be refused at the moment it starts. He rejects passion and keeps a form of love, friendship (amicitia), inside the wise person's life.

2. Plato, under the seven questions

12 min

Plato is a "classical thought" topic: there is no set text and no set translation, so the marks come from knowing who says what, in which dialogue, and what the argument is. Tap the gold terms for a definition. The seven headings below are the same seven you will meet for each of the other three voices, in the same order, so that the pages line up when you read across.

What love is

Socrates, questioning Agathon, establishes that love is always love of something, and of something one lacks: the tall man does not desire to be tall (Symposium 200a to 201c). Diotima then builds the doctrine: Eros is a spirit between god and mortal, child of Poverty and Resource, always poor and always scheming (203b to e), and love is "of the good being one's own for ever" (206a). The answer to "what is love" is therefore lack, and the thing finally lacked and wanted is Beauty itself (211). Keep the speaker straight: "Plato, through Diotima, argues that..." for the ascent; "Pausanias, one of Plato's speakers, argues that..." for the two Aphrodites; never "Plato says" for a view given to Agathon or Aristophanes, whom Socrates corrects.

Love and desire: the same or different?

For Plato eros is desire; the question is what it is desire for. Pausanias splits it in two: the Common Aphrodite's love is of women as much as boys and of the body rather than the soul, and the Heavenly Aphrodite's love is of the soul and lasts (180c to 185c). Diotima does not split it; she ranks its objects on a ladder, from one beautiful body to all bodies to souls, laws and knowledge, and finally to Beauty "pure and unmixed with human flesh" (210a to 212a). So the fault lies in wrongly directed desire, never in desire as such.

What desire does to the body

The Phaedrus gives the symptoms: the lover who sees a beautiful face shudders, sweats and feels heat; the wings of the soul begin to grow; in the beloved's absence the lover aches and cannot sleep, and in his presence the pain stops (251a to 252a). The point to make is that these are the same symptoms as Sappho 31, and Plato uses them as evidence about the soul: the body is reacting to the soul's recollection of true Beauty (249d to 250e). Alcibiades gives the same experience from the inside, the bite of Socrates' words in his heart and his own condition as slavery (217e to 219e).

Should desire be controlled, and how?

Yes, by reason, and Plato gives the picture everyone remembers: the soul as a charioteer with two horses, one obedient and one unruly; at the sight of the beloved the bad horse drags toward sexual pleasure and the charioteer pulls back on the reins until the horse is humbled (Phaedrus 253c to 254e). Republic 3 (402d to 403c) says the right kind of love has no sexual pleasure in it, and a lover may kiss and touch his beloved "as a father would a son" and no further. But the same Socrates calls the lover's madness the best of the divine madnesses (244a to 245c), so control is not suppression. The desire is kept and redirected upward.

Marriage

Marriage is the lowest answer to the desire for immortality. Diotima says the union of man and woman is a divine thing because it gives mortals a share of immortality through children (206c), but those "pregnant in soul" produce wisdom and poems, and anyone would rather have the children of Homer and Hesiod than human ones (208e to 209e). In the ideal city of Republic 5 there are no private marriages at all: the guardians' wives and children are held in common and mating is arranged at festivals by rigged lots (457c to 461e). Say that this is the ideal city, not advice to Athenians.

Men and women

The Symposium is a men's dinner. Pausanias ranks love of males above love of women (181c); Aristophanes says men descended from the male-male being are the best of their generation, and mentions women who love women in one sentence (191e). Women appear as examples (in Phaedrus's speech Alcestis dies for her husband, 179b) and as the absent authority Diotima, whose doctrine Socrates reports. Halperin asks why Diotima is a woman and answers that Plato gives the female power of childbirth to male philosophy as "pregnancy in soul". Dover supplies the custom behind Pausanias: the older partner pursued, the younger was not to show desire, and yielded only to gain virtue.

Good and bad conduct

Good conduct is love that leads to virtue: the youth may yield to a lover for the sake of becoming good himself, not for money or quickly (184c to e), and, in Phaedrus's speech, an army of lovers would be invincible because love makes people ashamed of disgrace (178e to 179a). Bad conduct is love of the body alone, the common lover who "takes wing and flies away" when the bloom fades (183d to e), and the lawless desires that wake in sleep when reason is off guard (Republic 9, 571c to 572b). Lysias' speech in the Phaedrus, that the non-lover is a better partner because the lover is mad and jealous, is stated (230e to 234c), repeated by Socrates (237a to 241d) and withdrawn in his second speech (243a onwards).

3. Sappho, under the seven questions

12 min

Sappho is a prescribed source, so the marks come from short quotations in the OCR wording with the Loeb number. The fragments below are the ones you studied in Lessons 1 to 7. Notice as you read that Sappho does not answer the seven questions in the way the philosophers do: she describes, and the description is the answer.

What love is

The nearest she comes to a definition is Fragment 130: "Love which loosens the limbs Once again shakes me! Bitter-sweet, invincible creature that he is." Eros is a living agent, he returns ("once again"), he is sweet then bitter (Carson: glukupikron, sweet first), and he cannot be fought. Fragment 16 gives a definition by preference: "The most beautiful sight in the whole world... I think it is the one you love." Fragment 47 gives the force: "Love shook my soul, Like a wind buffeting oak trees On a mountain."

Love and desire: the same or different?

Sappho does not separate them. The longing is the love: "She remembers gentle Atthis, And the longing consumes her flighty soul!" (96). Fragment 94 puts "on a soft bed... You satisfied your desire" in the same list as groves and dances, and Fragment 112 says to the groom "Honey-sweet, love is poured Over your beautiful face". The one fragment that ranks something above the physical is 50: "A handsome man is only good to look at, A good man will become handsome", the closest Sappho comes to Plato's move from body to soul.

What desire does to the body

Fragment 31 is the catalogue: "My tongue breaks", "A delicate fire runs beneath my skin", "I see nothing with my eyes", "a buzzing in my ears", "sweat pours over me", "a tremor seizes me all over", "I am greener than grass", "On the point of death". The beloved is present; the man beside her is the foil. Three readings: jealousy of the man (Page), the man as the third point of a triangle that shows how far the speaker is from the woman (Carson), the man as a set-up to be dismissed (Winkler). "Greener than grass" is pallor, not envy.

Should desire be controlled, and how?

Control is not the question Sappho asks. Desire is petitioned, not governed: Fragment 1 asks Aphrodite not to "overpower my heart with anguish" and to "Be my ally", and the goddess's reply, "if she does not love you now, she will love you soon even if she does not want to", treats desire as something the god does to people. Fragment 130's "once again" says it keeps coming back. The speaker never reins anything in; she asks for help, remembers, curses, or waits. Fragment 137 is the one check: "I want to say something, But a sense of shame holds me back", and the reply says shame only stops shameful speech.

Marriage

The wedding songs give both sides. Celebration: "Raise the roof, Hymenaeus!... The bridegroom is coming, Like Ares" (111); "Lucky bridegroom... You have the girl of your dreams" and "Aphrodite has honoured you Above all others" (112). Cost: Hesperus brings "The child, to its mother" on the evening the daughter leaves (104a); the hyacinth "Trodden by the feet of the shepherds" (105c); "Virginity, virginity, where have you gone? You left me behind" (114). Seaford and Rehm read the Greek wedding as structured like a death for the bride; Karanika reads the mirrored lines as a girls' game that gives the loss a communal voice. Fragment 121 refuses an unequal match: "I never want to be the older one In a relationship."

Men and women

A woman's voice desiring women: 31, 94, 96, 1. duBois reads Fragment 16's Helen, who "Abandoned her husband, The most excellent of all men", as a woman who acts and chooses, set against the martial values of epic. The poems live in a community of women: the grove of Fragment 2, the shared "good times" of 94, "Now, for my companions, I will sing these songs beautifully" (160). Men appear as the groom, the doorkeeper with "size twenty-seven feet" (110), and "that man" in 31. Winkler calls this a double consciousness: Sappho knows the public male culture and writes from inside a private female one.

Good and bad conduct

Good conduct is tenderness kept across absence: "Go, farewell, and remember me" (94); the moon among the stars (96). Bad conduct is measured by refinement and loyalty, not by law: the woman with "no share Of the roses from the Pierian muses" whom "no one will ever remember" (55); the "farm girl, in her farming clothes" who "Charms your mind" (57); the rejection "Atthis, the thought of me Now repulses you and you run off to Andromeda" (131); the cold "I used to love you, Atthis" (49, a contested join). Sappho moralises once, in 137.

4. Ovid, under the seven questions

14 min

You have read Ars Amatoria 3 as far as Part VII (lines 1 to 348): the proem, the lessons on appearance, and the accomplishments. Everything below the first rule in the bookpage is drawn from those lines, in Kline's wording with the booklet's line numbers. A second, shaded table lists what Parts VIII to XVIII will add to each question, so that you can see the shape of the whole book now and fill the cells as you reach them. Two words before you start: the speaker is the praeceptor amoris, the teacher of love, a persona whom Ovid makes vain and inconsistent, so you write "the teacher says", not "Ovid believes"; and his pupil is not a wife (he says so at 483 and 613 to 616), which matters for the marriage question.

What love is

A technique. The four heroines destroyed by men were destroyed by one thing: "Not knowing how to love: your art was lacking: love lasts long through art" (3.41 to 42). Ars is the title word, and the lessons are in "the ways of being loved" (3.28), a passive: not how to love but how to be the object of a man's desire. Love is also a game: "Only playful passions will be learnt from me" (3.27), and the book will end with "The game is done" (3.809). Where Plato defines and Sappho describes, the teacher instructs.

Love and desire: the same or different?

Desire is the subject and "love" is the name for keeping it going. The proem's argument is to the body's clock: "Have fun while it's allowed, while your years are in their prime: the years go by like flowing waters" (3.61 to 62), and "pluck the flower, which, if not plucked, will of itself, shamefully, fall" (3.79 to 80). The teacher argues consent away: "Even if you're deceived, what do you lose? It's all intact: though a thousand use it, nothing's destroyed that way" (3.89 to 90). Gibson notes that the "nothing is lost" reasoning is a stock seducer's line from Greek comedy, so a reader who recognises it knows what kind of adviser this is.

What desire does to the body

In Sappho the body suffers desire; in Ovid the body is the raw material that produces it. The governing word is cultus: "good wine comes from vines that are looked after" (3.101), "Taking pains brings beauty: beauty neglected dies" (3.105), "Art imitates chance" (3.155), "art delights in its hidden face" (3.210). The woman is the fifth artwork after Myron's statue, the ring, the dress and the Venus: "many things that please when done, are ugly in the doing" (3.218). Then the catalogue of defects, "If you're short sit down, lest, standing, you seem to sit" (3.263), and even the laugh: "Who'd believe it? Girls must even learn to laugh" (3.281). The one moment desire breaks into the teacher's own body is 3.309 to 310: "when I see it, I want to kiss your shoulder, as far as it's shown." Downing calls the method anti-Pygmalion: the woman sculpts herself, under the man's direction, so that the man remains the viewer.

Should desire be controlled, and how?

By manners, for effect. "There's measure here as in most things: both the rustic's stride, and the more affected step should be foregone" (3.305 to 306): Gibson's middle way between the matron and the prostitute, not too plain and not too gaudy. Tears are taught ("They're taught to cry, with propriety", 3.291 to 292), the lisp is taught, silence is taught ("Charm's in a defect", 3.295). The control is of the performance. The desire itself is left alone, because the desire is the product. Merriam's reading of the laughter passage is the sharpest statement: the teacher cannot forbid women to laugh at men, so he persuades them that laughing is ugly, and the beauty advice is the management of male anxiety.

Marriage

Listed and dismissed. The faithful wives, Penelope, Laodameia, Alcestis and Evadne (3.15 to 22), prove that women can be virtuous, and at line 25 the teacher says virtue is not his subject. Childbirth is priced as damage: "the time of youth is shortened by childbirth: the field's exhausted by continual harvest" (3.81 to 82), against the Augustan law that rewarded a mother of three. Andromache in "healthy clothes" and Ajax's wife in "seven hides of an ox" (3.109 to 112) are devoted wives turned into fashion failures. The teacher's assurances that he stays within "modesty, principles and your rules" (3.58) and does not "say sell it" (3.97) keep the married woman just outside the poem.

Men and women

The teacher says he is making the fight fair: "Go equal to the fight" (3.3), "It's not fair for armed men to battle with naked girls" (3.5), and "Men often cheat: it's seldom tender girls" (3.31). Then the lesson is in being the prize. He addresses "men" at line 6 before he has said a word to the women, and speaks as a man throughout: "wealth which you court us with, often makes us flee" (3.132), "We're captivated by elegance" (3.133), "our hair, snatched at by time" (3.161). Women are Sirens who "could restrain a ship's course as they wished" (3.311 to 312), and a woman's voice "a better procuress than your looks" (3.316). James's thesis is that the real audience of a book for women is the men being shown the tricks, and that the teacher "lets the women see too much" by telling them to read Books 1 and 2 (3.341 to 342).

Good and bad conduct

Good conduct is successful conduct. The goddesses who took mortal lovers are the examples to follow (3.87 to 88); refusing is the one error: "So why should any woman say: 'Not now'?" (3.95). The reading list ends with "let Sappho be yours (well what's more wanton?)" (3.331), which is the teacher's one line on Sappho and reads her as an erotic entertainer. Volk's point is that the full machinery of a teaching poem, the commission from Venus, the catalogues, the sailing metaphors, is real didactic form spent on hairstyles; the comedy is in the gap between the form and its use.

5. Seneca, a first sketch

12 minNew

You have not studied Seneca yet, and this page is not the full treatment. It gives you enough to put him in the fourth column so that the grid is complete and the comparisons can be practised now. Everything here will be deepened when we teach him properly; the references are the ones you will meet again. Seneca is a "classical thought" topic like Plato: no set text, marks for naming the work and stating the idea precisely.

Who he was

Lucius Annaeus Seneca, born in Spain around 4 BC, a Stoic philosopher, playwright and politician. Tutor to the young Nero and then his adviser; exiled by Claudius; forced to kill himself in AD 65 after a conspiracy. His wife Paulina tried to die with him. His works on our topic are the Letters to Lucilius, the dialogues On Anger, On the Happy Life and On Tranquillity, a lost book On Marriage known from Jerome's use of it, and the tragedy Phaedra.

The Stoic framework

Virtue is the only good; everything else, including health, wealth, marriage and children, is an "indifferent", though some are "preferred". A passion is a mistaken judgement that something external is good or bad, plus an impulse to act, and it only becomes a passion when the mind assents to the impression. The four cardinal passions are cupiditas (desire), pleasure, fear and grief. The goal is apatheia, freedom from them, which is not numbness: the wise person keeps rational feelings and keeps friendship.

Why "anti fun" is only half right

Seneca rejects pleasure (voluptas) as a goal: "Pleasure is a low thing, slavish, weak, perishable" (On the Happy Life 7.3). But he does not reject love. The wise man wants a friend and loves him (Letter 9), and the surviving account of his own marriage is affectionate. What he refuses is passion: desire that has taken over the mind.

How the paper has used him

Paired with Ovid in 2022 (guidance to women) and 2024 (desire as threatening); with Plato in 2025 (were their ideals of love different); named in 2023 ("Seneca fears desire"). The 2024 mark scheme's Seneca points: excessive desire as "a festering wound, threatening to health"; the difference between desire as a want and as a need; his marriage to Paulina; love described as a friendship.

What love is

Love worth having is friendship, amicitia, a rational attachment: the wise man is content with himself and still wants a friend, not to be served by him but to serve him; he will love his friends, and he bears the loss of a friend with an even mind, because he carries everything he needs within himself (Letter 9, in paraphrase; the OCR topic has no set translation). Love pursued as pleasure is a species of cupiditas, a passion, and shares pleasure's low address: "the brothels and the taverns" (On the Happy Life 7.3).

Love and desire: the same or different?

Different in kind. Desire is a passion, a false judgement that a future good is worth wanting; love of a friend or a wife is a rational feeling that the wise person keeps. The difference between a want and a need is the 2024 mark scheme's phrase for it. Where Plato ranks desire's objects on a ladder, Seneca separates the passion from the attachment and keeps only the attachment.

What desire does to the body

The tragedy Phaedra gives the case study. Phaedra, consumed by desire for her stepson, says: "What can reason do? Madness has conquered and now reigns" (vicit ac regnat furor, 184 to 185); just before it she admits that her mind goes headlong knowing what it does and comes back in vain looking for sound counsel (177 to 184). The Nurse reports the symptoms: she neither eats nor sleeps, her colour has gone, she weeps and cannot keep still (360 to 386), and Phaedra herself later says the fire rages deep in her marrow and runs through her veins (640 to 644). The body's first reactions, the shiver at a sound, the tears at a sad sight, are "first movements" (primi motus) and are not in our power; the passion begins only when the mind assents (On Anger 2.2 to 2.4). So the symptoms of Sappho 31 and Phaedrus 251 are, for Seneca, the body's prelude; the fault is the assent that follows.

Should desire be controlled, and how?

Yes, and early. Phaedra's Nurse states the doctrine: "Whoever resists love at the first and drives it out is safe and the victor; whoever nurses the sweet evil by indulging it refuses too late to bear the yoke he has taken on" (Phaedra 132 to 135). Refuse the assent at the first movement; there is no moderate passion: "Our school drives them all out. The Peripatetics moderate them. I cannot see how any moderation of a disease can be healthy" (Letter 116.1). Rehearse loss in advance so that nothing surprises you (praemeditatio, On Tranquillity 11). The contrast with Plato is exact: Plato redirects desire upward through the charioteer's struggle; Seneca refuses it at the door.

Marriage

Marriage is a preferred indifferent: the wise man marries, but passion for one's own wife is still a passion. The maxim preserved by Jerome: "the man who is too ardent a lover of his own wife is an adulterer" (Against Jovinian 1.49). Jerome gives the line to Sextus the Pythagorean; scholars place it among the material from Seneca's lost On Marriage that Jerome was using, so say that it reaches us through a hostile, much later source and treat it as evidence of the Stoic ideal. The positive picture is Seneca's own: "My wife Paulina is anxious for me; and because I know that her breath depends on mine, I begin, in caring for her, to care for myself... for her sake I command myself to grow old" (Letter 104.1 to 5). Tacitus records that at his death she opened her own veins and was saved on Nero's order (Annals 15.63 to 64). This is love redirected from passion to partnership, not the absence of love.

Men and women

Seneca works inside the Roman vocabulary of virtus: the question is self-mastery, and for a citizen the role that nature assigns. Letter 95.20 to 21 attacks women who have matched men's licence in drink and vice and who, "born to be passive", take the male part; Letter 122.7 lists among those who live against nature the men who exchange their dress with women and keep a boy's smoothness past its time. In both the moral failure, in Stoic terms, is the mind's assent to what nature rejects. Treat this as evidence of Roman elite discourse and name the framework; do not import modern approval or condemnation into AO1. A wife is a partner in virtue, and the mother Seneca praises in the Consolation to Helvia never painted her face or wore revealing dress. Edwards reads his moralising as performance indexed to anxieties about elite masculinity.

Good and bad conduct

Good conduct is governing oneself: temperantia, refusing assent, loving with judgement. Bad conduct is any surrender to passion, in the brothel or in the marriage bed. The man who governs his desire is, in this discourse, the image of the man fit to govern the state (Griffin). The hypocrisy charge, that the courtier of Nero preached what he could not live, is the standard evaluation point against him, and the standard reply is that the tension between strict doctrine and Roman practice is the philosophical project itself.

7. Where they agree and where they disagree

14 min

Six pairs, because the paper sets pairs. Each pair has three lists: where the two writers agree, where they disagree, and where it depends which passage you choose. The third list is the one that earns the top band, because that is where evaluation comes from: the writers are not consistent, and showing that you know it is AO2.

Plato and Sappho

Agree
  • Love is a desire for what one lacks: Socrates' argument (200a to 201c) and Fragment 16's turn to Anactoria "Who is not here with me". Carson's reading of Sappho and Diotima's Eros, child of Poverty, say the same thing.
  • The body registers desire first: Phaedrus 251a and Fragment 31 give the same symptoms.
  • Something outlasts the body: Fragment 50 ("A good man will become handsome") and Fragment 55 (the Muses' roses) are Sappho's nearest moves toward soul and immortality.
Disagree
  • What to do with the lack: Plato's lover climbs away from the one body (210a to 212a); Sappho's speaker stays with the one named woman. Vlastos says Plato's theory cannot love a whole person; Sappho loves "her lovely walk, And her gleaming face".
  • Who speaks: in Plato the desiring subject is male and the woman's doctrine is reported; in Sappho a woman desires in her own voice (duBois).
  • Define against describe: Plato wants an account of eros; Sappho gives its effects and never asks what it is.
Depends on the passage
  • Is Plato for or against desire? The charioteer (253c) and Republic 3 say rein it; the divine madness (244a) and Alcibiades say it is the most alive thing in the dialogue (Nussbaum).
  • Is Sappho warning or celebrating? Fragment 31 ends "On the point of death"; Fragment 94 remembers desire satisfied on "a soft bed"; the June 2022 question turned on this.

Plato and Ovid

Agree
  • Desire is produced by lack and distance. Diotima's Eros is always poor; the teacher will manufacture the lack on purpose in Part XIV (†"What's easily given nourishes love poorly", 579).
  • Love is something you can be taught to do well: Diotima's "right way" of loving (210a) and the teacher's "ways of being loved" (3.28) are both curricula, even if their destinations are opposite.
  • Fame outlasts the body: Diotima's children of Homer (209c) and the teacher's "What do sacred poets seek but fame?" (†403) and his own name on the shelf (3.339 to 348).
Disagree
  • The direction of travel: Plato's ladder leaves the body behind; the teacher keeps love on the bottom rung and makes even that a matter of technique.
  • Control: Plato controls the desire by reason; the teacher controls the performance by manners and leaves the desire exactly where it is.
  • What love is for: virtue and immortality against pleasure and a game ("Only playful passions will be learnt from me", 3.27).
Depends on the passage
  • Does Ovid know anything about the soul? The catalogue of defects says no; the games section (†369 to 380, "in a game the naked heart's exposed") treats the woman as a person with a character for twelve lines.
  • Is the teacher's praise of fairness real? "Go equal to the fight" (3.3) against "the ways of being loved" (3.28), two lines apart.

Sappho and Ovid

Agree
  • Love is bodily and visible: the trembling, the pallor, the walk, the laugh. Both poets write about the same features of a beloved.
  • Adornment matters: Fragment 81's garlands for Dika and Fragment 98a's headband sit beside the hair lessons of 3.133 to 168. Kurke's refinement (habrosyne) is a value in both worlds.
  • Youth passes: Fragment 121 ("I never want to be the older one") and the teacher's "the years go by like flowing waters" (3.62).
Disagree
  • Who does the loving: Sappho's speaker suffers desire ("Love... shakes me"); the teacher's pupil produces it in someone else ("Art imitates chance").
  • The beloved's features: in Sappho nothing about the beloved is a defect; the Ovidian pupil learns to see her own body as a list of faults (3.263 to 278).
  • The laugh: Fragment 31 loves "charming laughter" for its power over the speaker; the teacher muffles it (3.281 to 290) for its power over him (Merriam).
  • Sincerity: Sappho's "Honestly, I want to die" (94) against taught tears (3.291).
Depends on the passage
  • Does either poet celebrate desire? Sappho 130 calls it "invincible" and 31 ends near death; the teacher's carpe diem is a celebration and also a sales technique.
  • Is the Ovidian advice useless to a woman? The hair menu and the catalogue of defects are the most practical material in the unit (Gibson: technical, inclusive); the June 2022 mark scheme credited exactly that.

Plato and Seneca

Agree
  • Desire untreated overwhelms reason, and the body shows it first: Phaedrus 251a and Phaedra 360 to 386 describe the same symptoms.
  • Love is rescued by being redirected, not abolished: Plato toward Beauty, Seneca toward the friend's virtue and the wise person's self-sufficiency.
  • The philosopher's self-control is the model: Socrates refusing Alcibiades (219c to d) and the wise man of Letter 9.
  • Marriage is below the highest love: the lowest rung of immortality for Diotima, a preferred indifferent for Seneca.
Disagree
  • The soul: Plato's is in three parts and reason fights appetite (the two horses); Seneca's mind is one thing, and passion is its own mistaken judgement.
  • Partial success: Plato honours the lover who does not quite ascend (Phaedrus 256c to d); Seneca rejects moderate passion as moderate disease (Letter 116).
  • Where the goal is: metaphysical (the Form of Beauty) against ethical (virtue, apatheia).
  • Same-sex love: Pausanias and the Phaedrus make the male pair the privileged form; Seneca's frame is Roman role and virtus (Letters 95 and 122).
Depends on the passage
  • Whether Plato "encourages" desire: the ladder begins with one body and the madness is divine, but Republic 3 and 9 sound as strict as Seneca.
  • Whether Seneca is cold: Letter 116 and the Jerome maxim against Letter 104 on Paulina.

Seneca and Sappho

Agree
  • What desire does: Fragment 31's catalogue and the Nurse's report of Phaedra (360 to 386) are the same clinical picture.
  • Desire returns and is hard to fight: "Once again shakes me! Bitter-sweet, invincible" (130) is the condition the Nurse warns about ("refuses too late to bear the yoke").
  • Something is worth more than wealth or looks: Fragment 148 ("Wealth without virtue can be a harmful neighbour") and Fragment 50 are lines Seneca could sign.
Disagree
  • The response: Sappho petitions Aphrodite and submits; Seneca refuses assent and governs.
  • Is it a judgement? For Seneca the passion is a false belief one can decline; for Sappho eros is "invincible", a god, not a mistake.
  • Memory: Fragment 94's "remember me" keeps the attachment alive; the wise man bears a friend's loss with an even mind.
  • Marriage: Sappho's wedding songs pour Aphrodite's honey over the groom's face (112); Seneca's husband must not love his wife too ardently.
Depends on the passage
  • Whether Sappho celebrates desire at all: 94 and 2 yes; 31, 1 and 130 record it as suffering, which is nearer to Seneca than the June 2023 statement allows.
  • Whether Seneca fears desire: Phaedra and the Nurse yes; Letter 104 shows affection commanding self-care, which is not fear.

Seneca and Ovid

Agree
  • Time is short and must be used: Letter 1 and the teacher's "Life's to be used: life slips by on swift feet" (3.65) share a premise and draw opposite conclusions.

Disagree

8. How to write the comparison

12 min

The grid gives you the content of a comparison. This section gives you its shape. A comparative paragraph has five rungs, in order, and the rung most candidates miss is the fourth, where the second writer enters. The three model paragraphs that follow answer three real questions, one for each kind of pairing the paper sets, so that you can see the same five rungs carrying different material.

1. Point
A claim that answers the question and already contains the comparison: "Sappho and the teacher both write about a woman's laugh, and they want opposite things from it."
2. Source
A short quotation in the OCR wording with its number, or a precise paraphrase with the dialogue or letter named, woven into your sentence.
3. Analysis
Name the technique or the argument and explain its effect. This is AO2. A scholar's idea can be used here, with the scholar named, and then tested against the text.
4. Comparison
The hinge sentence: "whereas", "by contrast", "where Plato..., Sappho...". The second writer gets a quotation or reference of their own, not a summary. Say why they differ: genre, purpose, audience, philosophy.
5. Evaluation
A judgement with a reason: which presentation is more convincing, more realistic, more honest, and because of what. A second scholar, agreed with or argued against, belongs here.

Failure 1: the list

"Plato thinks love is lack. Sappho thinks love is a force. Ovid thinks love is a game." Three true sentences and no comparison. The examiner cannot award AO2 because nothing has been related to anything. Fix: a hinge sentence that puts two writers in one sentence and says what the difference means.

Failure 2: false symmetry

"Both Sappho and Seneca describe the symptoms of desire." True, and it stops where the interesting part begins. Fix: after the agreement, the disagreement: they describe the same symptoms and respond in opposite ways, and the reason is that one is a lyric poet reporting and the other a Stoic prescribing.

Failure 3: no judgement

Two writers compared accurately, and the paragraph ends. The 30-mark grid wants evaluation: which view is more convincing, and why. Fix: one sentence of judgement with "because", and ideally a scholar you agree or disagree with.

The question's own words

Every real question has a loaded word: "warns", "celebrate", "fears", "realistically", "education", "necessary", "different". Define it in the first paragraph and test each writer against it. "Seneca fears desire" is where the 2023 essay starts, and the top answers showed that "fears" is the wrong verb for a man who thinks desire is a mistake.

Three model paragraphs: the same five rungs, three real questions

Each paragraph is a body paragraph, not an introduction. Read it, then read the anatomy table under it, which shows where each rung falls.

Model paragraph 1. June 2023, 30 marks: "Seneca fears desire, but Sappho and Ovid celebrate it." (Sappho and Seneca)

The statement is wrong about Sappho before it is wrong about Seneca, because the fragment that comes nearest to defining desire records it as an assault rather than a pleasure: "Love which loosens the limbs Once again shakes me! Bitter-sweet, invincible creature that he is" (Fragment 130). The verb is violent, the word order puts the sweetness first and the bitterness second (Carson's point that glukupikron describes the order of the experience), and "invincible" concedes the fight before it starts; this is not a celebration but a report from the losing side. Seneca would recognise every symptom, since his Phaedra says "Madness has conquered and now reigns" (Phaedra 184) and her Nurse reports that she neither eats nor sleeps (360 to 386), whereas he would refuse Sappho's conclusion: for the Stoic the "invincible creature" is a false judgement the mind has assented to, and the Nurse's rule that "Whoever resists love at the first and drives it out is safe and the victor" turns Sappho's "Once again" into a failure of discipline rather than the act of a god. The difference is one of genre and purpose: a lyric poet performing for her companions has no reason to prescribe, and a philosopher writing to Lucilius has no other reason to write. Sappho's account is the more honest of the two about what desire feels like from inside, because Seneca's own tragedy has to admit, through Phaedra, that the moment of assent is not experienced as a choice; but "fears" is still the wrong verb for Seneca, whose position is that there is nothing to fear in a judgement one can decline.

Model paragraph 2. June 2025, 30 marks: "The poems of Sappho give a much better education about love and relationships for women than those of Ovid." (Sappho and Ovid)

The clearest test of the statement is what each poet does with a woman's laugh, because both treat it as powerful and only one treats it as hers. In Fragment 31 the beloved's "charming laughter" is what "strikes terror into the heart in my breast": the laugh belongs to the woman who laughs, and its effect on the listener is the whole subject of the poem, told from the listener's side. The teacher in Ars 3 hears the same power and sets out to remove it: "Who'd believe it? Girls must even learn to laugh" (3.281), after which the laugh is reduced to "a small dimple either side" with "the teeth mostly concealed by the lips" (3.283 to 284) and the woman who laughs freely is "like a mangy ass braying at the shameful mill" (3.290). Merriam's reading explains the difference: in elegy the woman and the gods of love laugh at the lover from a position of power, and the teacher cannot forbid the laugh, so he persuades women that it is ugly, which means the beauty advice is the management of a man's fear. Where Sappho's poem educates a woman by giving her desire a voice, the teacher's lesson educates her into a performance scripted by its audience, and the difference is one of purpose: Sappho sings "for my companions" (160), the teacher speaks of "us" and "we" throughout a book addressed to women (3.132, 133). The statement is therefore largely right, but "education" needs qualifying, since Gibson is also right that the teacher's advice on hair, dress and defects is the most practical material in the unit; what Sappho offers is not instruction at all but the record of a woman's experience, which is a different kind of education and, for the question's purposes, the better one.

Model paragraph 3. June 2025, 30 marks: Discuss to what extent you agree that Plato's and Seneca's ideals of love were different. (Plato and Seneca)

The two philosophers describe the same emergency and prescribe different rescues, which makes their ideals less different than their methods. Plato's picture is the charioteer whose unruly horse "drags toward sexual pleasure" at the sight of the beloved until the driver pulls back on the reins (Phaedrus 253c to 254e): the soul has parts, reason fights appetite, and the struggle is itself the beginning of the ascent, since the lover who wins approaches the beloved "with reverence" and the one who partly fails still "gains something" (256c to d). Seneca has no horse to fight, because for the Stoic the mind is one thing and a passion is its own mistaken judgement: desire begins not with the sight of the beloved but with the assent that follows it (On Anger 2.2 to 2.4), so the only rescue is to resist "at the first", as the Nurse puts it, and there is no honourable partial success, since "Our school drives them all out. The Peripatetics moderate them" (Letter 116.1). Where Plato redirects desire upward and keeps it, Seneca declines it and keeps only the rational attachment of friendship, the wise man who wants a friend in order to serve him and bears his loss with an even mind (Letter 9), and the difference is one of destination: Beauty itself for the one, the friend's virtue and the wise man's self-sufficiency for the other. Both ideals, though, rescue love by moving it away from the body, both rank marriage below the highest love, and both make the philosopher's self-control the model, so the statement is right about the mechanism and wrong about the aim. Nussbaum's reading of Alcibiades, that Plato shows the reader the man who could not climb, suggests Plato is the more honest about the cost of the ideal; Seneca's Letter 104 on Paulina suggests he is the more livable.

Strong

The statement is true of Sappho only if "emotion" means the suffering of desire, because that is what her poems record: "Love which loosens the limbs Once again shakes me!" (130), "Honestly, I want to die" (94). What the wedding songs add is the emotion a relationship is built to hold, "Honey-sweet, love is poured Over your beautiful face" (112), so for Sappho a successful marriage is one over which Aphrodite presides, and the feeling is the point. Seneca would say the opposite about the same feeling and the same institution: the man who is "too ardent a lover of his own wife is an adulterer" (via Jerome), because passion is a false judgement wherever it occurs; yet his own letter about Paulina, "for her sake I command myself to grow old" (Letter 104), shows a marriage held together by an attachment he does not call a passion, so Seneca thinks a rational feeling is necessary and a passionate one fatal.
